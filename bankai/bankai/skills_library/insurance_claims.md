WHEN TO USE: Filing, documenting, or appealing an insurance claim — health, home, auto, disability, life — and any time a claim is denied, underpaid, or stalled.
Also use immediately after a loss event (water, fire, theft, collision, hospitalization) to set up documentation before evidence disappears.

# Insurance claims and appeals

## The core fact

A first denial is a **first offer**, not a verdict. A large share of denials are
reversed on internal appeal, and a further share on independent external review.
Insurers price on the assumption that most people stop after one "no". The
household's advantage is entirely procedural: documentation, deadlines, and
escalating to whoever is required by law to answer.

## Documentation habits (do these before you need them)

- **Photograph the whole house annually** — every room, open closets and
  cabinets, serial-number plates on appliances and electronics, the roof, the
  garage. Store off-site (the vault counts). A post-loss inventory built from
  memory is where claims die.
- **Keep the declarations page, full policy PDF, and endorsements** for every
  policy in the vault, with renewal dates. The *policy*, not the summary, is
  the contract; the household almost never has it.
- **Know four numbers per policy:** coverage limits (dwelling A, contents C,
  loss-of-use D), deductible (flat vs. percentage — hurricane/wind/quake
  deductibles are percentages of dwelling value), replacement cost vs. actual
  cash value, and any sublimits (jewelry, cash, business property, water backup).
- Save receipts for capital improvements — they justify higher rebuild costs.

## First 72 hours after a loss

1. **Mitigate.** Every policy imposes a duty to prevent further damage. Tarp,
   water-extract, board up. Keep receipts — mitigation costs are reimbursable.
   Do **not** make permanent repairs before inspection.
2. **Photograph and video everything before touching it.** Wide shots plus
   close-ups plus the water line/burn pattern. Timestamped.
3. **Report promptly.** Late notice is the cleanest denial an insurer gets.
4. **Start a claim log** — every call, with date, time, name, title, phone
   extension, claim number, and what was said. Follow every substantive call
   with a same-day email: *"Confirming our call today: you stated X. Please
   correct me in writing if that is inaccurate."* This converts verbal
   representations into evidence.
5. **Keep damaged property** until told in writing it can be discarded.
6. **Sign nothing** from a contractor that assigns benefits or grants a lien
   without reading it. "Assignment of Benefits" agreements hand your claim to a
   stranger.

## Deadline discipline — the clocks that actually matter

- **Proof of loss:** commonly due 60 days after the insurer's request. Missing it
  is a stand-alone ground for denial. If you need more time, request an
  extension **in writing** and get the extension in writing.
- **Suit-limitation clause:** most property policies shorten the time to sue to
  1–2 years from the date of loss — far shorter than the general statute of
  limitations. Calendar it the day the claim opens.
- **Health plan internal appeal:** generally 180 days from the denial notice to
  file. Urgent/expedited requests get a much faster decision (72 hours or less
  for urgent care).
- **External review** under the ACA: generally 4 months after the final internal
  denial, decided by an Independent Review Organization whose decision **binds
  the plan**. This is the single strongest consumer lever in health insurance and
  it is free.
- **State prompt-pay statutes** set deadlines for acknowledging, investigating,
  and paying claims (often 15/30/45 days). Cite them by name in escalations.
- Put every one of these in the app as a rule/reminder the day the claim opens.

## Read the denial letter like a lawyer

The letter must state a reason. Sort it into one of four buckets, because the
appeal differs entirely:

1. **Administrative** — wrong code, missing prior authorization, wrong provider
   NPI, coordination-of-benefits questionnaire never returned. Fix and resubmit;
   this is not an appeal, it is a correction, and it resolves a large share of
   "denials".
2. **Medical necessity / experimental** — needs a clinical rebuttal: letter of
   medical necessity from the treating physician, chart notes, guidelines
   (specialty society, NCCN, MCG/InterQual criteria), and peer-reviewed
   literature. Ask for a **peer-to-peer review** between your physician and the
   plan's medical director — often faster than a written appeal.
3. **Coverage/exclusion** — quote the policy language back. Ambiguity in an
   insurance contract is construed against the drafter (*contra proferentem*).
   Demand the specific policy provision and page relied upon.
4. **Valuation/scope** — they agree it is covered but underpay. This is an
   estimate fight: get your own licensed contractor's line-item estimate, compare
   it against theirs line by line, and invoke the **appraisal clause** in the
   policy (each side names an appraiser, they pick an umpire). Appraisal is
   binding on amount, not coverage, and is much cheaper than litigation.

Also **demand the claim file**: many states require the insurer to provide the
adjuster's notes, engineer's report, and estimate. Ask for the full report if an
"independent" engineer was used — those reports are frequently the whole case.

## Appeal letter structure

Keep it to two pages, numbered and unemotional.

1. **Header block:** policy number, claim number, date of loss, insured name,
   "FORMAL APPEAL — [First Level / Second Level]", and the denial letter date.
2. **One-sentence ask:** "I am appealing the denial dated March 3 and requesting
   payment of $14,320 for covered water damage under Coverage A."
3. **Facts** — chronological, dated, neutral. No adjectives.
4. **Why the denial is wrong** — quote their stated reason, then answer it with
   the policy language, the medical record, or the estimate line item. One
   numbered paragraph per reason given.
5. **Evidence list** — enumerated exhibits, attached and labeled (A, B, C).
6. **The demand and the deadline:** "Please respond in writing within 30 days.
   If the denial is upheld, provide the specific policy provision relied upon and
   instructions for external review / the next appeal level."
7. **Preservation line:** "Please preserve all records relating to this claim,
   including adjuster notes and any engineering or vendor reports."

Send by a method that produces proof of delivery, and email a copy.

## Escalation ladder

1. Adjuster → **claims supervisor/manager** (ask by title; get their name).
2. **Second-level internal appeal** if the policy or plan offers one.
3. **Independent external review** (health) or **appraisal** (property).
4. **State Department of Insurance complaint.** Free, online, and consequential:
   the insurer must respond to the regulator in writing within a set window
   (commonly 15–30 days), and complaint volume affects market-conduct exams.
   Attach the whole claim log. This resolves stalled and lowball claims
   remarkably often. For self-funded employer health plans, the DOI has no
   jurisdiction — go to the **US Department of Labor / EBSA** instead (ERISA).
5. **Public adjuster** for large property losses — licensed, works on a
   percentage (often 5–15%, capped by state law); worth it above roughly
   $50k or when scope is the dispute. Check the license.
6. **Policyholder-side attorney.** Many states have **bad-faith** statutes and
   fee-shifting provisions (unreasonable delay/denial can expose the insurer to
   extra-contractual damages and your attorney's fees). Mentioning "unfair claims
   settlement practices" — the state's version of the NAIC model act — is the
   signal that you know the vocabulary.

## Signs a claim is wrongly denied

Reason shifts between letters; the denial cites no policy provision; the
"independent" engineer never entered the building; the estimate omits code
upgrades (ordinance-and-law coverage) or overhead and profit for a
multi-trade repair; depreciation withheld with no path to recover it under a
replacement-cost policy; a medical denial issued by a reviewer in a different
specialty; or contact simply stops. Every one of those is an escalation trigger,
not a reason to give up.

## Reporting back to the household

State the deadline that binds next, the single next action, and who owns it.
Never let a claim exist without a calendared date. Say plainly that you are not
a licensed adjuster or attorney and that a large or disputed loss warrants one.
