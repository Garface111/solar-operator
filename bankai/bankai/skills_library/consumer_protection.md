WHEN TO USE: Disputing a charge, fighting a debt collector, fixing a credit report, or responding to fraud on a card or bank account.
Also use whenever a company stonewalls the household and you need the statutory lever and deadline that forces a response.

# Consumer protection levers (FCBA, EFTA, FCRA, FDCPA)

## Pick the right statute — the rights are NOT the same

| Situation | Law | Key clock |
|---|---|---|
| Billing error on a **credit card** | FCBA (15 U.S.C. §1666) | Written dispute within **60 days** of the statement |
| Unauthorized/erroneous **debit card, ACH, or bank** transfer | EFTA / Reg E (§1693) | Report **within 2 business days** for the lowest liability cap |
| Wrong information on a **credit report** | FCRA (§1681) | Investigation within **30 days** of dispute |
| Being pursued by a **third-party collector** | FDCPA (§1692) | Written dispute within **30 days** of the validation notice |
| Merchant simply didn't deliver / bad goods | Card-network **chargeback** rules | Typically **120 days** from transaction or expected delivery |

**Credit cards are strictly safer than debit cards.** Under FCBA/Reg Z maximum
liability for unauthorized credit card use is $50 (and issuers routinely waive
it); under Reg E, debit-card liability escalates with delay and can become
unlimited. Advise the household to put anything disputable on a credit card.

## FCBA — credit card billing errors

Covers: charges you didn't make or authorize, wrong amount or date, goods not
delivered as agreed, goods not accepted, failure to post payments or credits,
computational errors, and statements not mailed to your current address.

Procedure that triggers the statute (a phone call does **not**):

1. Send a written "billing error notice" to the issuer's **billing inquiries**
   address (not the payment address — it's on the statement), so it arrives
   within 60 days of the statement containing the error.
2. The issuer must **acknowledge within 30 days** and **resolve within two
   billing cycles (max 90 days)**.
3. While it is pending you may **withhold payment on the disputed amount** and
   related finance charges, and the issuer may **not report it as delinquent**
   to credit bureaus or take collection action on that amount.
4. If they find no error, they must explain in writing and you may respond
   within 10 days to preserve the dispute's presence on your credit file.

**Claims and defenses (§1666i)** is the separate, underused lever for *quality*
disputes — the goods were defective or the service was never performed. It
technically requires the purchase to be over $50 and in your home state or
within 100 miles, but the major issuers routinely waive those conditions. You
must have made a good-faith attempt to resolve it with the merchant first —
document that attempt.

**Chargeback vs. FCBA:** a chargeback is a card-network contractual process
(Visa/Mastercard/Amex rules, typically 120 days from transaction or from the
expected delivery date, with longer windows for undelivered future services).
The FCBA is federal law with actual penalties. Use both: file the dispute in the
app/phone for speed, and send the written FCBA notice for legal protection.
Cancelling a subscription and getting billed anyway is a textbook chargeback
with reason code "cancelled recurring transaction" — bring the cancellation
confirmation.

## EFTA / Regulation E — debit cards and bank accounts

Liability tiers for unauthorized electronic fund transfers:

- Report within **2 business days** of learning of the loss/theft: capped at **$50**.
- After 2 business days but within **60 days** of the statement: capped at **$500**.
- Beyond **60 days** after the statement was sent: **unlimited** for transfers
  occurring after that window. This is the cliff that ruins people.

Bank duties: investigate within **10 business days** (20 for new accounts). If it
needs more time — up to 45 days (90 for POS, foreign-initiated, or new-account
transfers) — it must issue **provisional credit** within those 10 business days.
Always ask for provisional credit by name; it is not automatic in practice.

Also under Reg E: a household may **stop payment on a preauthorized recurring
debit** by notifying the bank at least 3 business days before the scheduled
date, and may **revoke authorization** with the company. A merchant that keeps
debiting after revocation is committing unauthorized transfers.

Note the hard limit: **money you were tricked into sending yourself** (authorized
push payments — Zelle, wires, P2P scams) is generally *not* covered as
"unauthorized". Say this plainly rather than promising a refund.

## FCRA — credit reports

- Free reports from all three bureaus at **AnnualCreditReport.com** (the only
  federally authorized source; verify the current free cadence).
- **Dispute in writing with the credit reporting agency** (Equifax, Experian,
  TransUnion) *and separately with the furnisher*. Online disputes are fast but
  produce thinner records — for anything serious, mail it with delivery proof.
- The CRA must investigate within **30 days** (45 if you supply extra information
  during the 30) and send written results plus a free copy of any changed report.
  Unverifiable information must be **deleted**.
- Include: identifying info, the exact tradeline, why it's wrong, what you want
  done, and copies (never originals) of proof. One letter per item.
- **Reinsertion** of deleted information requires written notice to you within 5
  business days — watch for it.
- **Retention limits:** most negative information falls off after **7 years**;
  Chapter 7 bankruptcy after **10**. A collection's clock runs from the original
  delinquency date, and **re-aging** that date is a violation.
- **Identity theft:** file at IdentityTheft.gov, then use §605B to demand the CRA
  **block** the fraudulent information — required within **4 business days**. Add
  a fraud alert (1 year, free) or a **security freeze** (free, indefinite, at all
  three bureaus; also consider ChexSystems, NCTUE, Innovis).
- Willful violations carry statutory damages of **$100–$1,000** plus punitive
  damages and attorney's fees. That fee-shifting is why FCRA letters get answered.

## FDCPA — third-party debt collectors

Applies to collectors and debt buyers, **not** to the original creditor
collecting its own debt (though state analogues often do cover them).

- The collector must send a **validation notice** within 5 days of first contact
  (the CFPB's Reg F model notice). You have **30 days** to dispute in writing;
  the collector must then **cease collection until it mails verification**.
- **Debt validation letter** — send by certified mail, return receipt, within the
  30 days. Ask for: the amount and itemization, the original creditor's name and
  address, the account number, the date of default/last payment, proof they own
  or are authorized to collect the debt, and their license number in your state.
  Do **not** admit the debt is yours, and do **not** make a payment — a payment
  can restart the statute of limitations on a time-barred debt.
- **Cease-communication letter (§1692c(c))**: after written notice, the collector
  must stop contacting you except to say it is stopping or that it is filing
  suit. Powerful, but it does not make the debt disappear and it can accelerate
  a lawsuit — use deliberately.
- Prohibited: calls before 8 a.m. or after 9 p.m. local time; contacting you at
  work after being told not to; contacting you when they know you have counsel;
  discussing the debt with third parties; threats, false statements about legal
  status, or claiming to be an attorney or government agent. Reg F adds a
  presumption of harassment at **more than 7 calls in 7 days** per debt, or a
  call within 7 days of a phone conversation about that debt.
- Remedies: actual damages, **statutory damages up to $1,000 per action**, costs
  and attorney's fees; **one-year statute of limitations** from the violation.
  Keep voicemails and log every call.
- **Never ignore a lawsuit.** A default judgment leads to garnishment and levy.
  Answer within the state deadline (often 20–30 days) and raise the statute of
  limitations if the debt is time-barred.

## The CFPB complaint as an escalation weapon

`consumerfinance.gov/complaint` — free, 10 minutes, covers banks, card issuers,
credit bureaus, collectors, mortgage and student loan servicers. The company is
routed the complaint and is expected to respond, usually within 15 days (60 max),
and the response becomes part of a public database. In practice this is the
fastest way to reach a company's **executive resolution team** — the same tier
that answers regulators — after front-line support has said no twice. Send it
after you have a written record, attach the timeline, and state the specific
remedy you want. Parallel escalations that raise the stakes: the **state
attorney general's consumer protection division**, the **OCC** (national banks),
the **NCUA** (federal credit unions), and the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov.

## Standing habits

Dispute in writing; keep proof of delivery; log every call with name, time, and
reference number; never dispute by phone alone; never accept a verbal
"pay-for-delete"; and calendar every statutory deadline the day a dispute opens.
Say plainly that this is process guidance, not legal advice — a lawsuit, a
judgment, or a five-figure dispute needs a consumer-protection attorney, and many
take FCRA/FDCPA cases on contingency because of fee-shifting.
