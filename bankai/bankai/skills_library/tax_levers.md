WHEN TO USE: Any question about reducing US household taxes — retirement contributions, HSA/FSA, Roth conversions, selling a home, energy upgrades, year-end moves.
Also use before December 31 planning conversations and whenever deciding if a CPA or EA is required rather than optional.

# US household tax levers

> ## ⚠️ EDUCATIONAL REFERENCE — NOT TAX ADVICE
> **Every dollar figure in the tax code is inflation-adjusted annually and
> Congress changes the rules constantly. This file deliberately contains almost
> no numbers. NEVER quote a contribution limit, phase-out, bracket, or credit
> amount from this document or from memory — look up the current-year figure
> (IRS Pub 590-A/B, Pub 969, Rev. Proc. notices, irs.gov) and say where you got
> it. If you cannot verify a number, say so instead of guessing.**
> **This is not tax advice. Consequential moves need a CPA or EA.**

## Order of operations (the standard household waterfall)

1. **401(k)/403(b) up to the full employer match.** An instant 50–100% return;
   nothing else competes. Verify the match formula *and* the vesting schedule.
2. **HSA to the max**, if on a qualifying HDHP.
3. **High-interest debt** above ~7–8% APR.
4. **Max the 401(k)** elective deferral.
5. **IRA / backdoor Roth.**
6. **Mega backdoor Roth**, if the plan allows after-tax contributions with
   in-plan Roth conversion or in-service withdrawal.
7. **Taxable brokerage**, managed for tax efficiency.

## 401(k) mechanics that matter

- The **elective deferral limit** is per person, not per plan; the much larger
  **overall annual additions limit** (employee + employer + after-tax) is per
  employer. Two jobs in one year = easy accidental over-deferral; catch it
  before the April deadline for a corrective distribution or it is taxed twice.
- **Catch-up contributions** start in the year you turn 50. SECURE 2.0 added an
  enhanced "super catch-up" window in the early 60s, and requires catch-ups to
  be *Roth* for higher-wage earners. Both rules have had delayed and phased
  effective dates — **verify what applies to the current tax year.**
- **Traditional vs. Roth deferral** is a bracket-arbitrage bet: traditional if
  today's marginal rate is higher than expected retirement rate, Roth if lower
  (early career, sabbatical year, low-income year, or when hedging future rates).
- **Solo 401(k) / SEP-IRA** for self-employment income allows both employee and
  employer contributions — often the single largest lever for a 1099 household.
- Leaving a job: rollover to an IRA is usually right, **but** a large traditional
  IRA balance sabotages the backdoor Roth (see pro-rata below). Consider rolling
  *into* the new employer's 401(k) instead.

## HSA — the only triple-tax-advantaged account

Deductible going in, grows tax-free, tax-free out for qualified medical
expenses. Requires HSA-qualified HDHP coverage; no other disqualifying coverage
(including a general-purpose FSA — a *limited-purpose* FSA is compatible).

- Contributions via **payroll deduction also escape FICA**; direct
  contributions do not. Prefer payroll.
- The **last-month rule** lets someone HDHP-eligible on December 1 contribute
  the full year's amount, with a 13-month testing period — failing it triggers
  income plus a penalty.
- **Stealth IRA move:** pay current medical costs out of pocket, invest the HSA,
  and keep every receipt indefinitely. There is no deadline to reimburse
  yourself for an expense incurred after the HSA was established.
- After 65, non-medical withdrawals are taxed as ordinary income with no
  penalty — an IRA with a medical superpower.
- **Not portable to a spouse's plan the way you think:** family coverage limits
  are shared; catch-up contributions are per-person and require each spouse's
  own HSA.

## FSA / DCFSA

- **Health FSA** is use-it-or-lose-it, softened by *either* a carryover *or* a
  grace period (the plan picks one, or neither). Elections are locked absent a
  qualifying life event. Fund it to the floor of expected spending, not the cap.
- **Dependent Care FSA** is often better than the child and dependent care
  credit for higher-bracket households — but you cannot double-count the same
  expenses. Run both computations.
- FSA money is available in full on day one of the plan year; DCFSA is not.

## Backdoor Roth (concept)

For households over the Roth IRA income phase-out: contribute to a
**non-deductible traditional IRA**, then **convert to Roth**. Two things decide
whether it works:

1. **The pro-rata rule (IRC §408(d)(2)).** Every traditional/SEP/SIMPLE IRA you
   own is treated as one pot on December 31; the conversion is taxed
   proportionally to pre-tax basis across all of them. A large rollover IRA makes
   the backdoor expensive. Fix: roll pre-tax IRA money into an employer 401(k)
   *before* year-end.
2. **Form 8606**, filed for the non-deductible contribution *and* the
   conversion. Miss it and the same money gets taxed twice. This is the most
   common backdoor Roth error.

The step-transaction worry is generally considered resolved, but keep the
contribution and conversion clean and documented.

## Selling a home — §121 exclusion

Excludes a large amount of gain on a principal residence ($250k single /
$500k married filing jointly at last update — **verify**), if you owned **and**
used it as your main home for 2 of the last 5 years, and have not used the
exclusion in the prior 2 years.

- The two years need not be continuous; the ownership and use tests are separate
  (important for a spouse added to title).
- **Partial exclusions** are available for work relocation, health, or
  unforeseen circumstances — pro-rated by months.
- **Basis is the lever nobody tracks.** Capital improvements raise basis and
  shrink gain: additions, new roof, HVAC, windows, landscaping capital work,
  permits. Keep every receipt in the vault forever.
- Depreciation taken for rental or home-office use is recaptured and is **not**
  excludable.

## Energy and efficiency credits — VOLATILE, VERIFY

The residential energy credits (§25C Energy Efficient Home Improvement, §25D
Residential Clean Energy) and the clean-vehicle credits (§30D, §25E, §45W) were
**terminated on accelerated timelines by the 2025 budget law**, with
placed-in-service cutoffs that already passed for several of them. Do not
promise a household any energy credit without checking the current statute and
the placed-in-service date. Work already completed in an open tax year may still
be claimable — and an **amended return (Form 1040-X)** is available for
generally three years from filing. State and utility rebate programs are
separate from federal credits and often survive federal repeal.

## Other high-ROI levers

- **Tax-loss harvesting**, respecting the 30-day wash-sale window on both sides
  and across all accounts including a spouse's and IRAs.
- **Bunching** charitable gifts and deductible expenses into alternating years to
  clear the standard deduction; a **donor-advised fund** makes this one check.
- **Donating appreciated securities** instead of cash: deduct fair value, skip
  the capital gain.
- **QCDs from an IRA** after the qualifying age satisfy RMDs without hitting AGI.
- **529 plans** for state deductions; many states allow a deduction for
  contributions you immediately spend on tuition.
- **Saver's Credit** for lower-income working households — routinely missed.
- **Estimated-tax safe harbor:** pay in 100% (110% for higher earners) of last
  year's tax to avoid underpayment penalties regardless of what this year brings.
- **W-4 tuning:** a large refund is an interest-free loan to the Treasury.

## When a CPA or EA is mandatory, not optional

Stop and insist on a professional for: equity compensation (ISOs and AMT, RSU
withholding gaps, an **83(b) election — 30-day hard deadline, no extensions**);
any Roth conversion of size; rental property, depreciation, or real-estate
professional status; K-1s, partnerships, and S-corp reasonable compensation;
multi-state or part-year residency; foreign accounts or income (FBAR/FinCEN 114
and FATCA Form 8938 — penalties are severe); crypto with more than trivial
activity; inheritance, step-up in basis, and trust returns; divorce and QDROs;
NOLs or carryforwards; the year a business starts or sells; and **any IRS notice,
audit, or lien**. Give your full analysis, then say plainly that a licensed
professional must review it before they act.
